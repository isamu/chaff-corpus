# My father's tomatoes

My father grew tomatoes in the same corner of the backyard for thirty-one years. Same corner, same wooden stakes, which he repainted green every April whether they needed it or not. He died in February, and this spring I drove up to the house to decide what to do about the garden.

I wasn't planning to plant anything. I live in an apartment four hours away. I don't know how to grow tomatoes, and I'd told myself I would just clear out the beds so the place would look tidy when we put it on the market.

But in the garage, on the shelf above his workbench, there was a coffee can full of seeds. He'd labeled it in his blocky capital letters: BRANDYWINE — 2025. He had saved them from last summer's plants, the way he did every year. I'd seen him do it a hundred times, scooping the seeds out onto a paper towel on the kitchen windowsill, and I'd never once asked him why.

So I planted them. I called my aunt, who gardens, and she talked me through it over the phone. I started them in egg cartons on his windowsill, then put the seedlings in the ground in May, in his corner, next to his stakes. I didn't repaint them. That felt like his job.

I came up most weekends after that. I'm not sure I did it right. Some of the plants got a disease halfway through the summer that turned the leaves yellow, and my aunt said I'd watered them too much. But four of the six plants made it, and in August I picked the first ripe one, huge and lumpy and dark red, and ate it standing in the yard with salt from his kitchen.

I'd like to say it tasted like childhood, but mostly it tasted like a very good tomato. What got me was something else. Kneeling in that corner, I could see the whole yard the way he must have seen it every summer evening: the back of the house, the kitchen window, the spot where my mother used to hang the laundry. For thirty-one years, this was his view.

We've sold the house now. The new owners have two young kids, and they seemed pleased when I told them about the tomato corner. I don't know if they'll keep it up.

Before I handed over the keys, I scooped seeds out of the last tomato onto a paper towel and let them dry on the windowsill. They're in a jar in my kitchen now. I wrote the label in capital letters.
