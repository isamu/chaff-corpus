# Why I still write letters by hand

I have a drawer in my desk that's mostly envelopes. Some are from my grandmother, written in the slanted cursive they apparently taught everyone in the 1950s. A few are from a college friend who moved to Lisbon and wrote to me for about two years before we both drifted into texting. There's one from my dad, the only letter he ever wrote me, the week I left for my first job. It's four lines long. I've read it probably a hundred times.

I don't have a drawer like that for emails. I'm sure I've received thousands of kind ones. I couldn't tell you where any of them are.

That's most of why I still write letters, if I'm honest. Not because I think handwriting is morally superior, or because I'm trying to make some point about phones. It's because I've noticed what I keep, and it's paper.

---

It's slower, obviously. That's the annoying part and also the point. When I write a letter I can't backspace, so I think a little before each sentence. I end up saying fewer things, but they're closer to what I actually mean. In a text I'll fire off "haha that's so funny, miss you!!" and move on. On paper, "miss you" takes long enough to write that I find myself adding why: the specific Sunday I'm thinking of, the stupid joke from the car.

My handwriting is bad, by the way. Really bad. I cross things out. Once I spilled tea on a page and sent it anyway, with an arrow pointing to the stain that said "sorry, tea." My friend wrote back that it was her favorite part of the letter. I think that's something people get about handwritten letters even when they can't say it: the mess is proof that a person was there. You can see where I pressed harder, where I ran out of room and wrote up the margin, where I changed my mind.

---

I don't write as many as I'd like. Maybe one a month, sometimes fewer. I buy stamps in a book of twenty and it takes me most of a year to get through them. Some letters I never send. They sit half-finished under a coffee mug until whatever I was going to say has stopped being true.

And I know the obvious objection: who has time for this? Fair. I don't always. But it takes about twenty minutes to write a page, which is less than I'll spend on my phone tonight without noticing. It's not really about time. It's about which twenty minutes I want to keep.

Last spring, the friend in Lisbon sent me a postcard out of nowhere. Just a picture of a tram and three lines on the back. We hadn't written in eight years. I put it in the drawer with the others, and then I sat down and wrote her back.
