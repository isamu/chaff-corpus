# So what actually is a password manager?

A friend asked me this over dinner last week, after her email got hacked for the second time this year. I started explaining, realized I was waving my fork around a lot, and figured I should just write it down. So here's the version I wish someone had given me a few years ago.

## The problem it solves

Be honest: how many passwords do you actually have? Not accounts. Passwords.

If you're like most people (and like me, until a few years ago), the answer is "about three," and you rotate between them with small variations. Maybe a capital letter here, a "!" at the end there. It's not that you're careless. It's that nobody can remember 80 different strong passwords, and we all have something like 80 accounts.

The trouble is that websites get hacked all the time, and when they do, lists of email addresses and passwords end up floating around online. Bad actors take those lists and try the same email and password on everything else: your bank, your email, your Amazon account. If you reuse passwords, one leak from some forum you signed up for in 2016 can unlock a lot of doors.

## What a password manager does

A password manager is basically a locked notebook for your passwords, except it lives on your phone and computer and does the boring work for you.

- It **remembers** all your passwords so you don't have to.
- It **makes up** new ones that are long and random, like `t7#Rq!vZ29mLp`, which nobody is going to guess.
- It **fills them in** for you when you go to log in, so you never actually type them.

You lock the whole thing with one password, sometimes called a master password. That's the only one you have to remember. So yes, it needs to be a good one, but it's one, not 80.

## "Isn't it risky to keep them all in one place?"

This was my first question too, and it's a fair one.

The short answer is that the good password managers are built so that even the company that makes them can't see your passwords. Everything is scrambled (encrypted) on your device before it goes anywhere, and only your master password can unscramble it. If their servers got hacked, the thieves would get a pile of gibberish.

Is it perfect? No. Nothing is. But compare it to what most of us are doing now, which is reusing the same handful of passwords everywhere, or keeping them in a note on our phone called "passwords." The password manager wins by a mile.

## Which one should you use?

I'm not going to pretend there's one right answer, but here are the ones I'd point a friend to:

- **The one already on your phone.** If you have an iPhone, Apple's Passwords app is built in. If you use Chrome or an Android phone, Google Password Manager is already there. They're free and they work fine for a lot of people.
- **Bitwarden.** Free, works on everything, and it's what I use.
- **1Password.** Costs a few dollars a month, but it's very polished and great for families.

Honestly, the best one is the one you'll actually use.

## How to get started without it taking all weekend

You don't have to move everything over at once. That's how people give up.

1. Pick one and install it on your phone and computer.
2. Choose a strong master password. A good trick is to string together four or five random words, like "purple-otter-kettle-umbrella." Write it down and keep it somewhere safe at home until you've memorized it.
3. Next time you log in to something, let the password manager save it.
4. Start with your most important accounts: email first (since it can reset everything else), then your bank, then anything with your card saved.

Over a few weeks, most of your passwords will end up in there without much effort.

## One more thing

While you're at it, turn on two-step verification (the thing where you also get a code by text or an app) for your email and bank. Between that and a password manager, you're already safer than most people online.

My friend set hers up the next morning. She texted me a week later to say she'd changed 30 passwords and "felt weirdly smug about it." Honestly, that's the right feeling.
