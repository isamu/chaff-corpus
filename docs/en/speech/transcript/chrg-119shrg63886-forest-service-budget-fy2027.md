U.S. SENATOR FROM UTAH

The Chairman. Good morning. The Committee will come to

order. Today, we are going to hear from Tom Schultz, the Chief

of the United States Forest Service. We will be discussing the

Fiscal Year 2027 budget for the Forest Service. Welcome, Chief

Schultz.

To inform my colleagues on how we are going to proceed this

morning, I will begin with my opening statement. I will be

followed by an opening statement from Ranking Member Senator

Senator Wyden. Obviously dealing with some intel.

The Chairman. Very important stuff.

We will then turn to Chief Schultz after that, for his

prepared remarks, and that will be followed by questions from

Senators who will be recognized in order of seniority for those

present at the gavel and then in order of arrival following the

gavel.

So, Chief Schultz, welcome back to the Committee, and let

me also say, welcome to Utah, as far as the Forest Service

goes. You could not have chosen a better place for the U.S.

Forest Service headquarters than Salt Lake City, and I suspect

you will quickly come to appreciate why Utah's settlers looked

across the valley when they entered in 1847 and said: ``This is

the place.'' Utah is home to a vast amount of Forest Service

land. Nearly a quarter of the roughly 35 million acres of

federally owned land in our state is in the hands of the Forest

Service. Generations of Utahns have lived alongside those

beautiful landscapes, and cared for them, and helped preserve

the beauty and character that make them so remarkable today.

Western states uniquely understand the challenges facing

these lands and are keenly aware of the constant stewardship

that our forests require. For decades, however, the agency's

authority became concentrated far from the actual forests

themselves and even further from the communities that tend to

be most affected by the Forest Service's decisions. As the

bureaucracy expanded, decisions took longer to reach, active

management became more difficult, and local expertise was often

treated as secondary to directives coming from Washington, DC.

The consequences of that concentration have taken a heavy

toll--overgrown forests, crumbling infrastructure, growing fuel

loads, mounting maintenance backlogs, and increased wildfire

risk. These challenges are the entirely predictable

consequences of a philosophy that prioritizes preservation for

its own sake while neglecting the active management that

healthy forests require. Relocating the headquarters to Utah

acknowledges that these lands are best managed closest to the

people who live, work, and depend on them every day.

Despite these challenges, you have made very significant

progress. Rescinding the 2001 Roadless Rule is a perfect

example. For more than two decades, the Roadless Rule imposed a

rigid nationwide framework across many millions of acres of

Forest Service land that failed to account for local

conditions, wildfire threats, watershed needs, and the

realities facing so many of our western communities. The

inability to build or maintain roads has made restoration

projects more difficult, delayed fuel reduction work, limited

timber access, and complicated wildfire response efforts. All

the while, our forests grew denser, fuel loads accumulated,

insect infestation spread, and catastrophic fires became far

more severe. Healthy forests require active management. They

require thinning, timber harvest, fuel reduction, grazing,

watershed restoration, and strategies tailored to on-the-ground

conditions. The idea that forests can be left untouched has

proven unsustainable, and for many, dangerous.

Under your tenure, it has been refreshing to see the Forest

Service show a greater willingness to work alongside states and

localities rather than treating them as obstacles to just be

dealt with or managed around. The cooperative agreement signed

with Utah is an excellent example of what's possible when local

expertise and federal resources are aligned. And it's my hope

that this approach will continue to guide the agency moving

forward. Those types of partnerships matter, because the scale

of the challenge facing the agency cannot be overstated. The

Forest Service deferred maintenance backlog has increased by

more than 80 percent over the last five years. Roads, bridges,

trails, campgrounds, water systems, and other infrastructure

continue to age, while repair costs rise faster than projects

can be completed and the federal estate continues to grow.

Congress established the Legacy Restoration Fund to help

address that problem, and this budget proposes reauthorizing

it. I support efforts to reduce the backlog, but we also need

reforms that prevent the problem from continually compounding

year after year. No amount of spending will solve the problem

if the underlying management never changes. We cannot continue

expanding federal liabilities while roads, trails, campgrounds,

and critical infrastructure continue to fall deeper into

disrepair.

There are, of course, encouraging signs that the agency is

moving in a better direction. Timber targets are increasing.

Partnerships with western states are expanding. The Forest

Service is taking a more practical approach toward fuel

reduction, grazing, and restoration work. But substantial

challenges remain, and this budget hearing is an opportunity to

examine whether the agency is aligning its priorities and

resources with the realities as they exist on the ground. Our

national forests are indeed national assets, woven into the

story of the country itself. Keeping them strong for future

generations requires care, vigilance, and a recognition that

these lands will not remain healthy if they are simply left

unattended. The American people expect these forests to be

protected before disaster strikes, and not after.

I have the utmost confidence in your ability to navigate

these unique challenges, and I look forward to today's

discussion. So again, welcome, Chief.

I now recognize the Ranking Member, Senator Heinrich, for

his opening statement.

U.S. SENATOR FROM NEW MEXICO

Senator Heinrich. Thank you, Chairman Lee, and good

morning, Chief Schultz. Thank you for being here today to

discuss the Forest Service's Fiscal Year 2027 budget request.

Last year, I found the proposed budget deeply troubling.

And this year, when taken in the context of what have been a

relentless series of attacks on our public lands, the budget

seems disappointing and irresponsible. Over the last year, we

saw a chaotic hollowing out of the Forest Service staff, with

the firing--and rehiring, in some cases--of vital positions. In

addition, deferred resignations, early retirements. Ultimately,

we saw a loss of well over 5,000--roughly 5,800--Forest Service

employees. Now, I have heard from my constituents, who are

worried about the Forest Service's ability to carry out basic

responsibilities, everything from trail maintenance to projects

to protect infrastructure investments. We saw the Forest

Service pull back watershed protections in areas where local

communities are actually pleading for politicians in DC to

safeguard their water, both in my state and around the country.

We saw the Forest Service begin to strip away decades-long

habitat protections in backcountry areas, the very places that

I and my constituents go to hunt elk. On top of that, the

Forest Service continues to push forward with the

reorganization without a public-facing plan or assurances on

specifically how this will actually help the Forest Service

better manage our forests.

So, let's be clear: many of the claims that we will likely

hear today about how the Forest Service supports healthy

forests and partnerships with sportsmen, local communities, and

states don't match up to what is actually in the proposed

budget. For example, when talking about the reorganization, we

will hear about a desire to bring Forest Service managers

closer to the forests that they manage, something that I

support. However, workforce reductions proposed in the budget

mostly affect on-the-ground staff. These are the people who

actually carry out forest restoration and hazardous fuels work.

We will hear claims that the reorganization will not impact the

Forest Service's ability to continue to conduct top-notch

research, despite this budget actually proposing to eliminate

most of the research centers, something that I hope the Senate

and Congress will revisit.

So, let's look at the budget. The budget request eliminates

the Forest and Rangeland Research Program. To zero this program

out at a time when our forests face increasing threats from

wildfire, from insects, from disease, and from aridification is

shortsighted, and in my view, reckless. We will hear claims

about how eliminating regional offices will make the Forest

Service more responsive to state and local governments. I want

the Forest Service to be more responsive to state and local

governments, but in the budget, we see a total elimination of

the state, private, and tribal forestry programs. These

programs are important. They are vital for supporting forested

landscapes in the West, where land ownership is split among

federal, state, private, and tribal stewards. Eliminating these

programs would undermine non-federal forest management. And

claiming that states will somehow pick up the bill is

unrealistic. It's an abdication of our responsibility to manage

these lands today and for future generations.

And then there is the Wildland Fire Program. We are hearing

that the reorganization will not move the wildfire program out

of the Forest Service. So, let's look at the budget. Again,

with few details, and a passing recognition of the need for

future legislation, the budget proposes to move the Wildland

Fire Program to the Department of the Interior. Let me be

clear: when we are spending our time on make-believe budgets

and reorganizations and moving deck chairs around on the deck

of the ship, it means we are not preparing to fight fires,

which is my primary concern in this coming season, which looks

like it's going to be a doozy. When we're willing to lose

people who support the agency's ability to conduct research or

interact with our state and local partners, we are losing the

red card holders who make up incident command teams and the

fire militia. I said this last year, and I will say it again--I

am open to new ideas in wildfire policy, or better, more

efficient organization, but what we have been offered so far is

not thoughtful reform; it's a decoupling of the fire management

and land management missions. Those things should be done

together. They should be one and the same. That's how we have

holistic management of our forests.

I am also concerned that the Forest Service is claiming to

have achieved 90 percent of the hazardous fuel treatment goals

but, by their own account, the acres treated were down nearly

30 percent in 2025 compared with 2024. Given a budget proposal

that doesn't adequately support our communities, doesn't

effectively protect watersheds and wildlife habitat, and

doesn't seem to, at its core, value the people that carry out

that work, I have serious concerns about this administration's

ability to meet even the baseline land management

responsibilities. What this budget shows me is a road map on

how to dismantle the Forest Service. I want to empower the

Forest Service. I want them to be able to manage our forests.

Thank you, Chairman. I yield back, and I look forward to

our discussion today.

OPENING STATEMENT OF HON. TOM SCHULTZ, CHIEF, U.S. FOREST

SERVICE, U.S. DEPARTMENT OF AGRICULTURE

Mr. Schultz. Chairman Lee, Ranking Member Heinrich, and

members of the Subcommittee, thank you for inviting me here to

testify today.

Established in 1905, the USDA's Forest Service is the

nation's foremost federal forestry organization. We have the

privilege of managing 154 national forests and 20 national

grasslands, covering 193 million acres in 43 states and Puerto

Rico. The Forest Service works with communities, states and

local governments, tribes, forest industry, private forest

owners, NGO's, and others to manage for multiple uses, and

collectively, yes, we have shared challenges. Addressing

wildfire risk, threats to watersheds, infrastructure strain,

and the need to support rural economies that depend on healthy

forests and grasslands are top priorities for the Secretary and

me. None of these challenges stop at jurisdictional boundaries,

and none of them can be solved by the Federal Government acting

alone. They require partnerships with community and tribal

members who bring local knowledge, experience, and

accountability. The best outcomes happen when we plan and act

collectively, especially as the wildfire risk grows and

pressure on public lands increases.

My focus over the past year as Chief has been a return to

fundamentals--keeping people and communities safe, reducing

risk to communities, and actively managing forests and

grasslands so that they remain productive. Additionally, we

want to increase and improve public access for recreation and

other National Forest System uses. We talked about fire.

Wildfire readiness remains central to our mission, and year to

date, we are already seeing more fires. As of early May, we

have already hired about 10,500 firefighters, which is 11

percent more than we had done last year at this time, and eight

percent more than we had done in 2024. This is about a thousand

more firefighters on board this year compared to the same time

last year. Our target is 11,300 firefighters, which is the same

number we have had for the last several years.

From 2024 to 2025, non red-carded employees have increased

from more than 9,800 to over 10,300 employees. Bottom line,

overall, is that we will have more than 450 additional

responders available this year. Our firefighters are world-

renowned for their rapid response. This year, we will continue

to demonstrate the strength that keeps fires small and

communities safe, while also increasing the use of risk

reduction tools such as prescribed fire and mechanical

thinning. We have already thinned and used prescribed fire on

more than two million acres for this year to date. Just so you

know, last year was also predicted to be a bad fire season. We

had 12 percent more starts during the season, yet we burned

half as many acres. The Forest Service initial attack success

rate was the highest in 12 years. Last year, we were also very

safe. We reduced our OSHA recordable incidents by almost 15

percent from `24 to `25.

In much of the western United States, forests have

mortality on productive timberlands that now exceeds our growth

rates, particularly National Forest System. This is driven by

fires, insects, and disease. In other words, our forests are

dying faster than they are growing. The Forest Service

currently harvests timber on less than 0.5 percent of the total

forested acres every year. And despite our reserves of forest

land, we still rely heavily on Canada to meet our domestic

timber supply and our demand, as about 25 percent of the lumber

currently comes from Canada. Active forest management is not

optional. It's how we restore health to our forests, it's how

we reduce fire risk, and how we keep them open for public

access and productive. Expanding timber production, modernizing

grazing processes, and accelerating reforestation are all part

of restoring forest and grassland health and productivity.

Last year, we planted 285,000 acres of forests, and this

was more than we have done in the last 25 years. Our frontline

workforce is critical to expanding the management of the

national forest. This year, I committed to hiring 2,000

seasonal employees for the summer of `26, to ensure they are

able to do just that. We have made significant progress. We

have hired more than 880 seasonal employees so far this year to

date, and we are also looking to hire another 745. They are

going to start within the next four to eight weeks, which is

critical to our mission support.

Our national forests provide far more than clean water and

places to recreate. They are also working lands that support

America's energy security and innovation. Energy security is

national security. Responsible resource use strengthens our

nation's economy and resilience while upholding our multiple-

use mission. The Fiscal Year 2027 budget that the President

proposes focuses on Forest Service efforts to actively manage

the national forests for timber production, recreation, range,

critical minerals, and permitting for energy development.

Additionally, the budget emphasizes efficient and effective

fire management, building upon our current practices to unify

the federal suppression response apparatus into the U.S.

Wildland Fire Service under the Department of the Interior. The

unification of wildland fire under DOI, one bureau, will allow

the Forest Service to focus on its remaining core mission of

delivering critical outputs and services to the National Forest

System lands.

The 2027 budget firmly supports the President's timber

production goals by significantly increasing funding for active

management. The budget also proposes to increase the amount

retained by the agency from ranchers' and permittees' grazing

fees for reinvestment in National Forest System lands. Our

public lands offer some of the best recreational opportunities

in the world. National forest lands receive more than 160

million visitors annually, and we maintain safe, reliable

infrastructure for these visits and other management. It's

critical. That's why the President's budget requests

reauthorization of the Great American Outdoors Act. Similar to

the `26 request, the President's `27 budget reduces or

eliminates some aspects of federal funding in the Forest

Service budget to better balance appropriate roles of federal

and state governments.

In closing, the 2027 budget request underscores our

commitment to focus on investments and products for the

national forests that are critical to supporting rural

communities. We are prioritizing the fundamentals of managing

our forests for their intended purposes and ensuring maximum

value for the taxpayer. Thank you for inviting me here today. I

look forward to answering your questions.
