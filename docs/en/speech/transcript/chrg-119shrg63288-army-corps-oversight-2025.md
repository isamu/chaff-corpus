OPENING STATEMENT OF HON. SHELLEY MOORE CAPITO,

U.S. SENATOR FROM THE STATE OF WEST VIRGINIA

Senator Capito. All right, here we go. I am calling this

hearing to order, and welcome our witnesses. Thank you so much.

I apologize for the delay.

I also want to congratulate Adam Telle on his recent

confirmation. Hanging by a thread, there, Adam, until the very

end, but you made it across the finish line in our final vote

in August.

Today we will conduct oversight of the U.S. Army Corps of

Engineers Civil Works program and examine the agency's

implementation of prior Water Resources Development Acts. Since

2014, this committee has kept to a biannual schedule of passing

this bipartisan legislation and we look forward to keeping on

track with that record.

WRDA authorizes water resource studies and projects and

sets national priorities for our civil works program. The Corps

does critical work across the Nation through its navigation,

flood risk management and ecosystem restoration missions. This

work protects the lives and livelihoods of millions of

Americans, facilitates commerce throughout our Country and

internationally.

The timely implementation of these laws, consistent with

congressional intent, is essential. The Corps is actively

working on a number of studies and projects that I am going to

mention, because they are critically important to my State of

West Virginia.

The Lower Mud River Flood Risk Management project in

Milton, West Virginia, has been a priority of mine. In WRDA

2022, I was able to increase the Federal share of that project

from 65 percent to 90 percent. This change brought in the

support we needed without creating financial strain on our

State.

In 2023, I also secured an additional $190 million for this

project, bringing the total to $287 million in Federal funding,

delivering on the Federal side of the funding equation to make

this project happen. Last month, West Virginia signed an

amendment to the project partnership agreement to formalize the

new cost sharing agreement which allows the project to resume.

Secretary Telle, thank you for being there, I know that was

your first visit in your new official position, and I thank you

for joining us in Milton for that ceremony.

The State of West Virginia recently committed to matching

the Federal funds that I have secured for the Upper Guyandotte

and Kanawha River Basin Flood Risk Management studies. Just

over 9 years ago, in June 2016, West Virginia experienced

flooding at historic rates, leading to tragic deaths and

devastation.

The Kanawha River Basin study is a direct response to this

flooding. I championed a provision in WRDA 2024 that increases

the Federal cost share for future projects identified by this

study.

WRDA 2024 also increased the ability of the Corps to carry

out smaller projects for debris and obstruction removal, which

is critical to reducing the severity of flooding in many areas

of I am sure everybody's State, but particularly ours.

The fifth and final phase of the Bluestone Dam Safety

Assurance Mega-Project, in the city of Hinton, continues to

make significant progress. In September 2024, Hurricane Helene

led to high water levels in rivers and streams across the east

coast, well beyond the areas hit directly by the storm. The New

River was among those affected and resulted in historic water

levels against the Bluestone Dam.

The dam was able to withstand the rising waters and

prevented an estimated $577 million in damages. Once completed,

the project will protect more than 150,000 people and $20

billion worth of infrastructure.

Unfortunately, there is more to be done. This year alone,

several parts of West Virginia experienced devastating

flooding. In February, flooding in McDowell County resulted in

the loss of lives and necessitated major rescue and recovery

efforts. The damage wiped out the only route to the Welch

Community Hospital, and caused major infrastructure issues in

Wyoming and Mingo counties.

Ohio and Marion counties also experienced significant

damage and loss of lives following flash flooding on the

Father's Day weekend. My congressional colleagues and I

supported Governor Morrisey's requests for Presidential

disaster declarations, and I am grateful to President Trump for

his approval of these requests.

I will continue to work with the Corps and local government

officials to determine if additional authorities are needed to

help mitigate the impacts from future flood events.

Thank you again to the witnesses for being here. I now turn

to my Ranking Member, Ranking Member Whitehouse, for his

opening statement.

OPENING STATEMENT OF HON. SHELDON WHITEHOUSE,

U.S. SENATOR FROM THE STATE OF RHODE ISLAND

Senator Whitehouse. Thank you, Chairman, for this hearing

today. Thank you, Secretary Telle and Lieutenant General

Graham, for appearing.

As you know, one of my most vexing problems, one the Corps

has refused to remedy, is chronic underfunding of coastal storm

risk management projects. For my Ocean State, that is a bad

situation.

While flood risk is on the rise nationwide, coastal areas

face the most severe increases due to rising sea levels and

more frequent extreme rainfall events. In First Street's

National Flood Risk Assessment, coastal cities outrank inland

cities by all measures of substantial flood risk.

The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office has determined

that the share of properties exposed to flood risk in coastal

communities is more than twice that of inland communities, and

coastal communities are projected to see even sharper increases

in flood risk over the next two decades.

Greater flood and storm risks are, in turn, contributing to

a property insurance crisis that threatens to trash our entire

economy. When risk makes property uninsurable, the property

also becomes unmortgageable. Without mortgages, property

markets crash. As we all saw in 2008, when property markets

crash, so does the economy.

Despite this dire situation, coastal storm risk management

projects year after year receive between 20 and 100 times less

funding than inland flood risk management projects. That simply

is not good enough. The Corps needs to tackle problems like

this one head-on and embrace reform.

Assistant Secretary Telle, I supported your nomination and

confirmation because I know you understand and appreciate the

critical role that congressional oversight plays in ensuring

that Federal agencies deliver for people. I value your

commitment to maintaining an open line of communication with

Senator Capito and me, and particularly appreciate your

willingness to engage with committee staff on a regular basis

to keep the Corps accountable.

In prior testimony before this Committee, you spoke of your

formative experience helping to secure funding and resources

for the State of Mississippi's recovery from the devastating

effects of Hurricane Katrina. You know from experience managing

the FEMA portfolio for the Senate Homeland Security and

Governmental Affairs Committee that disasters do not

differentiate between red and blue States.

Under your leadership, I expect to remove the political

bias so blatant in the Corps' Fiscal Year 2025 Construction

Work Plan, which shifted over $250 million in construction

funding from blue states to red States, completely eliminating

funding for four critical California flood risk management

projects to fund navigation projects in Texas that did not even

make the cut in the Corps' Fiscal Year 2025 budget.

Assistant Secretary Telle, I expect you to put an end to

it. Egregious partisanship does not serve the Army Corps, and

it does not serve the American people.

I also expect to see progress on project delivery. Cost

escalations and delays are pervasive, recurring issues in the

Corps' civil works construction program. The Trump

administration's attacks on the Federal work force have only

made addressing these problems worse.

In just a few months, DOGE's deferred resignation program

and voluntary early retirement have hollowed out Corps offices,

so that across the agency, work force reductions undermine

mission execution. Some Corps district offices have lost as

much as 20 percent of their staff, and all have likely lost

expert personnel. The prolonged Trump hiring freeze is

exacerbating the staffing shortage.

As a result of this DOGE-driven chaos, Corps offices are

either deferring work, contracting it out at a higher cost, or

paying employees overtime to complete it, all while continuing

to pay salaries to employees who, under the DRP, no longer

report for duty.

This is not just a matter of work force morale; the DRP has

actually jeopardized mission-critical operations and increased

project costs.

Assistant Secretary Telle, General Graham, you have your

work cut out for you. I look forward to working with you and

hope that you will acknowledge the challenges, roll up your

sleeves, and work cooperatively with this Committee to address

them.

Hegseth's, and Secretary Driscoll's top official responsible

for overseeing the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' civil works

mission.

I was nominated by President Trump on March 24th of this

year, appeared at a hearing in May, and I was confirmed by the

Senate, as Chairman Capito pointed out, on August 2d. I began

work in this role immediately thereafter and have been on the

job for six and a half weeks. During this time, I have been

working alongside General Graham and his team to ensure that

the Army Civil Works program is positioned to deliver on

President Trump's vision for our Country and on its critical

mission, which this committee has had an important role in

defining.

As I said during the consideration of my nomination before

this committee, I am honored to help lead the Army Civil Works

mission. It touches small and large communities throughout the

Country, and very few government agencies' missions capture the

interests of American citizens, landowners, communities,

States, job creators, and the representatives here in

Washington quite like this one.

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers originated in 1775, before

the Declaration of Independence was even signed. The America we

know today developed, in large part, as a result of the work of

the Corps.

Congress has given the Corps two main missions in addition

to its original navigation mission: flood damage reduction and

ensuring the future availability of our aquatic resources.

Because the Civil Works program is important to the American

public and their elected representatives here in Washington,

during my first weeks, I have been working to establish greater

consistency and accountability across the four layers of this

enterprise, which consist of my office and the Corps

headquarters, divisions and districts.

I intend to spend my time in this job ensuring that the

Corps carries out its missions to benefit the American public

as effectively, as quickly, and as efficiently as possible. We

must optimize our program for quality, speed, and cost if we

want to do our part to protect lives and commerce as President

Trump is delivering on his vision to ensure that the United

States remains the greatest nation in the history of the world

during the 21st Century.

Our partners here on Capitol Hill, and particularly on this

Committee, have been pivotal in supporting this enterprise, and

I request your partnership as I work with General Graham to

ensure the Civil Works program evolves to become more efficient

and consistent to meet our modern demands. To deliver against

that workload, we must work in partnership with the Congress,

throughout the executive branch, and with our stakeholders to

drive efficiency, collaboration, and results in an environment

that is necessarily resource-constrained.

In addition to driving more efficiency into the Civil Works

program, we must continue to lift the Federal regulatory burden

on the public. This Administration is committed to ending the

last five decades of confusing and ever-changing definitions of

the term ``Waters of the United States'' under the Clean Water

Act of 1972.

Together with Administrator Zeldin and President Trump's

Environmental Protection Agency, the Army has already issued

guidance that follows the Supreme Court's Sackett decision, and

we will move quickly to issue a permanent definition that

follows the post-Sackett law of the land, reduces red tape,

cuts overall permitting costs, lowers the cost of doing

business in communities across the Country and continues to

protect America's waters.

In July, my office made much-needed changes to our National

Environmental Policy Act implementing procedures that will

reform, modernize, and expedite the Corps' environmental

reviews, eliminate unnecessary delays, and help ensure the

growth of highly cost effective, reliable infrastructure

projects across the Nation.

We also recognize the opportunities provided through WRDA

2024 and previous WRDAs, which this committee has had such an

important role in shaping. These efforts were intended to

address navigation, reservoir management, the beneficial use of

dredged material and many other issues.

I look forward to gaining insight today about your views

and interests, so that together, we can serve America's vital

infrastructure needs.

STATEMENT OF LIEUTENANT GENERAL WILLIAM H. GRAHAM, JR., 56TH

CHIEF OF ENGINEERS AND COMMANDING GENERAL, DEPARTMENT OF THE

ARMY, U.S. ARMY CORPS OF ENGINEERS

General Graham. Chairman Capito, Ranking Member Whitehouse,

members of the Committee, I am very honored to testify before

you today in regard to oversight of the U.S. Army Corps of

Engineers Civil Works program and implementation of the Water

Resources Development Acts.

Through the Civil Works Program, the Army Corps works our

partners to study, build and operate water resource projects

focusing on our three main mission areas of commercial

navigation, flood and storm damage reduction, and aquatic

ecosystem restoration. Let me break those down.

The navigation program, which consists of our coastal and

Great Lakes channels, as well as the inland rivers and inter-

coastal waterways, underpins much of the national economy,

ensuring that commodities can move reliably and efficiently.

Our flood and storm reduction program maintains 746 dams, over

13,000 miles of Federal levees and 450 miles of shore

protection. Those systems provide, on average, a staggering

$200 billion per year of flood damages avoided.

To continuously maintain and improve these mission areas, I

certainly want to thank this committee, as was mentioned by the

Chairman, for supporting the Corps' Civil Works program with

your commitment, since 2014, to passing biannual Water

Resources Development Acts. Those acts have provided us with

the authorities necessary to address the changing needs of the

Nation's water resources infrastructure.

WRDA 2024 advanced the Corps' principal civil works

missions by authorizing new navigation, flood risk management,

coastal storm risk management, and aquatic ecosystem

restoration studies and projects all across the Country. These

studies and projects, when funded, will strengthen the supply

chain, better protect Americans from flooding and improve

degraded aquatic environments.

We deeply value the authorities you have entrusted to us.

Our pledge is to put them to work to deliver for the American

people. To that end, the Army Corps' objective every day is to

safely deliver quality projects on schedule within budget. We

measure this closely. Our scheduled delivery, our current on-

schedule rate when I checked this morning was 80.4 percent.

That is a low, low, low B. That is unacceptable.

This performance is influenced by factors we can control

and factors such as inflation that we do not control. Focusing

on those things that we can control, the Army Corps is

absolutely committed to procedural improvements, as Mr. Telle

spoke about, in the following three areas.

First, we have to get the engineering right. Focus here is

on the majority of the designs. We will not request authority

to build new projects unless the design is advanced enough that

we truly understand what we are proposing to build. That

threshold is usually around the 35 percent level. We have not

been doing that in the past.

There are some nuances on how we need to work to break down

large, complex projects and there are some nuances on cost-

sharing of studies vice cost-sharing of construction. We are

committed to working through those nuances with this committee.

Fundamentally, fundamentally, we need to better understand

what it is we are proposing to build before we ask you for

authority to build it.

The second thing, we have to get the project management

right. Here we are focusing on crafting logical, fully

resourced schedules and sticking to them, ensuring that we are

delivering these important projects with a great sense of

urgency, or as General Kelly puts it, hustle. If we stay on

schedule, then we are likely to stay on budget.

Third thing, we have to get the team right. We are striving

to bring together the right mix of talent with either onboard

manning or leveraging the incredible talents in the private

sector.

In closing, the path to successful delivery is clear: plan

projects thoroughly, fund them efficiently; and build them

rapidly with the right team of master builders. I look forward

to working with this committee to stay on that path.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify today. I look

forward to addressing your questions.
